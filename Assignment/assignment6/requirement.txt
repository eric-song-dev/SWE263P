You should identify the top 4 usability heuristics from your workbook to present in your video. Then, record a 5-7 minute video essay to present an analysis of the usability of the interface with respect to the four heuristics you have chosen.

Video Content
Though we will not collect them, it is highly recommended that you create a set of slides to provide supporting visual content for your video. An easy way to produce the video recording is to open a Zoom meeting, share your screen, and record the meeting as you present the slides.

Good submissions will provide an introduction with a brief description of the system and summary of the video essay intent. The body of the video essay should consist of analysis organized by the four heuristics you chose, with separate slides/visuals for each. For each heuristic, define the principle and provide a reference to an authoritative source for the definition. Then, describe how the system adheres to or departs from that principle. Your video essay should make use of live demo, screenshots with markup, or other supporting audio/video/illustration to informatively and persuasively communicate your points. End with a summative conclusion.

Tips for Creating Effective Visual Aids
In UX design, visual representations are a critical form of communication and about design. However, using visuals to make a persuasive argument is not as straight forward as you might assume; it is a skill that requires development. Below are some tips that can help you earn top points on your submission's visual aids presentation.

Use your words. Do not expect a screenshot or photo to speak for itself. A phrase like, "as can be seen in the screenshot..." is meaningless (and inaccessible) without additional details. For example, in a live demo or using a screenshot of a Canvas page, you might say, "the blue navigation pane along the left displays several icons which link to various features, including the Dashboard, Courses, and a Calendar." In a live demo, you should additionally narrate my actions and system responses, like this: "If I click on Courses, you see a submenu slides out from the left."
Use annotations and zoom in. Circles, arrows, highlights, and text can all be added to your slide to indicate where readers should look. When important details of your visuals are too small to read without digital zoom, you need to magnify all or part of the image. Here's a great article with examples of how to present informative screenshots using annotations and zoom. Remember that annotations need to be verbalized in your video presentation.
Create a drawing. Sometimes a photograph or screenshot has too much distracting detail. A simple drawing can abstract away the unimportant features and highlight those you are interested in. For example, a "wireframe" sketch is a way to show the content areas of a webpage without filling in all the content (here are some examples).

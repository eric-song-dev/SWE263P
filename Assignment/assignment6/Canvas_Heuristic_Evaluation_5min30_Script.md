# Canvas Heuristic Evaluation Presentation Script

Target length: about 5 to 6 minutes at a natural pace.

## Slide 1 - Title

Hi everyone. I'm Zhenyu Song. Today I am presenting a heuristic evaluation of Canvas, focusing on the student side.

I looked at common tasks: finding assignments, checking due dates, reading announcements, viewing grades, using the calendar, and moving around course pages.

My main question is: how well does Canvas help students find what they need and decide what to do next?

## Slide 2 - Method

For the method, I used a heuristic evaluation. This means I inspected the interface using Nielsen Norman Group's usability heuristics. This is not a user test.

I used screenshots from UCI Canvas. In the workbook, I evaluated all ten heuristics. But in this video, I focus on four important ones.

## Slide 3 - System Overview

Canvas is a hub for student work. It brings together courses, deadlines, announcements, grades, and calendar events. Overall, it is familiar and mostly easy to learn, but important information is sometimes present without being easy enough to notice.

## Slide 4 - H1: Visibility of System Status

The first heuristic is visibility of system status. In simple words, the system should tell users what is happening.

Canvas does this well in several places. The To Do list shows upcoming work. Assignments show due dates. The Grades page shows the total grade and assignment weights.

The green box on the grade panel is a strength: it shows the total grade clearly. But the orange box shows a To Do item where the assignment name is cut off.

So Canvas has useful status cues, but some meaning is easy to miss. My recommendation is to label icon-only states and show full names for important To Do items.

## Slide 5 - H4: Consistency and Standards

The second heuristic is consistency and standards. Users should not have to guess whether similar icons, words, or actions mean the same thing.

Canvas is strong in its basic structure. The global navigation stays on the left. Course navigation is consistent. Blue text usually means a link. Grades use a familiar table layout.

The weaker part is icon meaning. Small icons appear in course cards, assignment rows, and grade rows. In these screenshots, labels are not visible when students are making decisions.

My recommendation is to standardize icon meanings across pages, and add labels or tooltips when the icon matters.

## Slide 6 - H6: Recognition Rather Than Recall

The third heuristic is recognition rather than recall. The interface should make options visible, so users do not have to remember too much.

Canvas supports this with persistent navigation. Students can see Home, Announcements, Assignments, Grades, People, and other course tools.

But the Dashboard adds recall burden. The orange boxes show truncated course names and icon-only shortcuts. If labels are hidden, students must remember what the icons mean.

My recommendation is to reveal full names on hover or focus, and add labels or a small legend for course-card shortcuts.

## Slide 7 - H8: Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

The fourth heuristic is aesthetic and minimalist design. This does not mean the page should be empty. It means extra information should not compete with what matters most.

Canvas has many useful things on screen: course cards, images, icons, To Do lists, weekly buttons, and side panels.

The problem is visual priority. Urgent work, images, navigation, and secondary tools can all feel equally important. Students may need to scan several areas before answering: what should I do next?

My recommendation is to make urgent coursework stand out more, offer a denser list view, and move secondary tools away from the main content.

## Slide 8 - Prioritized Recommendations

So I would prioritize four changes.

First, label status icons, such as comments, hidden scores, unread markers, and course-card shortcuts.

Second, reduce truncation. Important course and assignment names should be easier to read.

Third, show clear recovery steps. The search no-results page is a good example. It says there are zero results, repeats the search term, and suggests better keywords. Canvas should use this pattern for dismissed To Do items, hidden calendar events, and confusing grade states.

Fourth, add short contextual help near grades, hidden scores, calendar filters, and assignment status.

## Slide 9 - Conclusion

To conclude, Canvas is powerful and mostly learnable. Students can usually find the main pages they need.

The main problem is not missing information. The problem is that meaning can be hidden in small icons, cut-off labels, or dense layouts.

My overall finding is: Canvas works, but it should make status, meaning, and priority easier to see when students decide what to do next.
